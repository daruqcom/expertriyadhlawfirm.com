Family Business & Succession Lawyers in Riyadh

A founder assumes that their children, who've grown up around the business and clearly care about it, will naturally figure out leadership and ownership between themselves once the time comes, without ever formalizing who actually decides what or how disagreements between siblings running the business together should be resolved — an assumption that works fine while the founder is still actively present to informally mediate, but that frequently unravels into genuine conflict the moment the founder is no longer there to play that role. Family business and succession covers structuring governance, ownership, and leadership transition for a family-owned business, so it survives the transition from one generation to the next. Why family business succession needs deliberate planning

A family business that grows successfully often outgrows the informal decision-making that worked when the founder ran everything personally, and without deliberate succession planning, the transition to the next generation is where many family businesses run into serious difficulty.

This involves clarifying ownership structure, formalizing governance roles for family members involved in the business, and setting out a clear process for how leadership actually transitions when the time comes. The entity-level restructuring — holding companies, share transfers — runs through our corporate structuring practice.

Balancing family and business considerations

Succession planning has to work on two levels at once — sound governance for the business itself, and a process the family can genuinely live with, since a technically sound plan that ignores family dynamics rarely survives contact with reality.

This practice works closely with families to understand both dimensions, drafting governance documents that are legally sound and realistic given the specific family's relationships and expectations.

Why informal assumptions about the next generation rarely hold up

A founder's informal confidence that adult children will simply work things out between themselves once succession actually happens often rests on the founder's own ongoing presence as an informal mediator and tie-breaker — a role that disappears entirely the moment succession actually occurs, at precisely the point when formal structure is needed most and informal goodwill alone is least able to resolve genuine disagreement between siblings who may have very different visions for the business.

We help founders formalize decision-making authority, ownership stakes, and dispute-resolution mechanisms specifically so the business doesn't depend on the founder's continued informal presence to function smoothly, which is exactly the dependency that succession planning needs to remove well before it's actually tested by a real transition. The personal-estate layer of the same plan — the founder's own shares — is settled through wills & waqf and, ultimately, inheritance rules.

Why bringing in non-family expertise sometimes strengthens, not threatens, the transition

Family businesses sometimes resist involving non-family executives or independent board members during succession, worried this dilutes family control, when in practice a well-structured role for outside expertise can provide exactly the objective perspective and professional discipline a family transition often needs, without requiring the family to give up ultimate ownership or control of the business itself. Where the generational transition has already broken down into conflict, the matter moves to partner & shareholder disputes.

We help families think through where outside expertise genuinely adds value during a transition — professional management expertise, independent governance perspective, objective mediation of family disagreements — as a complement to family leadership, not a threat to it or a replacement for genuine family involvement in the business's direction.

Direct Answers

When should a family business start succession planning?

Earlier than most founders think — waiting until a health issue or unexpected event forces the question tends to produce a worse outcome than planning proactively.

Does succession planning mean the founder has to give up control immediately?

No — a good succession plan can include a gradual transition, with the founder retaining significant involvement for a defined period while the next generation takes on increasing responsibility.

How does this coordinate with inheritance planning for the family's other assets?

Closely — where a family's wealth is tied up in the business, succession planning and personal estate planning need to work together as one coordinated picture.

Should we just trust that our children will figure out leadership between themselves?

We'd recommend against relying on this alone — informal arrangements often depend on the founder's ongoing presence as a mediator, a role that disappears exactly when formal structure is needed most.

Will bringing in non-family executives mean losing family control of the business?

Not necessarily — a well-structured role for outside expertise can complement family leadership rather than replace it, providing objective perspective without requiring the family to give up ultimate control.

What's the biggest mistake families make in succession planning?

Waiting too long, and assuming informal family goodwill will substitute for formal governance structure — both tend to produce a harder transition than proactive, deliberate planning would have.
